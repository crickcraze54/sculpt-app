# Complete Hypertrophy Program — 6-Day PPL

Oct 22, 2026 · @Muhammad Ali

This is a 6-day Push/Pull/Legs ×2 built for anatomical completeness rather than taper, with roughly 20 hard sets per session, direct neck, forearm, tibialis, adductor and abductor work, and most axially-loaded barbell work replaced by supported or machine variants to keep intra-abdominal pressure low.

## Assumptions and where I disagree with your brief

Four things in your brief are wrong or unworkable as written, and the program below is built on the corrected versions.

**What I assumed.** You didn't give these, so change anything that doesn't match and I'll revise: intermediate natural lifter (your word); full commercial gym with cables, plate-loaded machines, a hack or pendulum squat, seated *and* lying leg curls, and adductor/abductor machines; 75–95 minutes per session including warm-up; eating at maintenance or a slight surplus, not dieting; no current shoulder, knee or lower-back injury beyond the reflux history. You also referenced a current program but didn't attach one, so there was nothing for me to anchor on.

**1. "Every region maximally developed" is not a reachable target, and chasing it costs you size.** The binding constraint on a natural lifter is weekly recovery capacity, not available session slots. Training six days instead of four redistributes volume; it does not create more of it. Completeness means every region clears its own growth threshold — not that every region gets the same 16–20 sets. Tibialis, deep neck flexors and wrist extensors look finished on 4–6 hard sets a week. Lats and lateral delts need three times that. A program that gives all of them 12 sets is simultaneously wasting work and under-dosing the muscles that decide your silhouette.

**2. Separation and detail are not training variables.** Intramuscular separation is produced by three things: body fat, the absolute size of the two muscles either side of the groove, and the fascial and aponeurotic anatomy you inherited. No grip, rep range, cable angle or "peak contraction" creates it. The only training lever available is making each adjacent muscle bigger so the valley between them is deeper — which is just hypertrophy again. Practically: striations start appearing around 10% body fat, deep separation around 7–8%, and you cannot hold that year-round naturally while still gaining tissue. Your detail goal is a nutrition and timeline goal wearing training clothes. I've built for size and balance; leanness is a separate project.

**3. Four of the regions you listed should not get their own exercise, and I'm not giving them one.** Vastus intermedius lies underneath rectus femoris with no independent moment arm — nothing selectively loads it, and it grows with everything that extends the knee. Gluteus minimus sits deep to medius, shares its actions, and has no access route distinct from abduction work. Transverse abdominis is not visible at any body fat and has no demonstrated hypertrophy relevance to appearance; dedicated "activation" drills for it are a rehab idea that migrated into bodybuilding without evidence. Pronator teres contributes almost nothing to visible forearm mass. Keeping these on the list produces exercise bloat and nothing else.

**4. Your exercise wish list, honoured literally, is a 2.5-hour session.** Every region you named, given its own movement, every day it appears, lands at 28–32 sets per session. The back half of that is junk volume performed tired. Each session below runs 7–9 exercises and roughly 18–22 hard working sets, with small regions rotated across days rather than repeated in every session.

**One thing you're half-right about.** You said you don't want a taper-focused program. Agreed on the training emphasis — but an anatomically complete physique *is* taper-shaped, because lats and delts have far more growth potential than waist structures do. You don't need to chase the shape; it arrives as a by-product. The failure mode you're correctly avoiding is training only the muscles that produce it.

**Medical.** I am not a physician and this is not clearance to train. The hiatal hernia modifications throughout are risk-reduction reasoning from mechanism, not from trial evidence — that evidence largely doesn't exist. Get a gastroenterologist's sign-off before loading anything heavy, and read the stop-signal list near the end before your first session.

## What a complete physique means anatomically

A physique reads as finished when no silhouette has a flat segment and no lit surface has an empty patch — which is a different and harder target than being big.

Three things are being judged at once, and they fail in different ways.

**Outline.** This is the shape against the background, and it is decided by a short list of muscles that sit at the edge of the body: lateral deltoid and upper lat at the top, the sweep of vastus lateralis and the gastrocnemius bellies at the bottom, and the long head of triceps and biceps on the arm. Outline failures are visible at distance and in clothing. Most of what people mean by "he's big" is outline.

**Mass in the depth axis.** The front and back views hide this entirely, and the side view exposes it in one second. The relevant structures are chest thickness from sternum to spine, the erector columns and mid-trap mass that stop the upper back looking like a plank, glute depth, hamstring thickness, and the neck. Almost everyone training for appearance under-builds this axis, because they train facing a mirror.

**Surface articulation.** This is what you called detail. Mechanically it is the visibility of the borders between adjacent muscles and of the tendinous intersections inside them. It requires the two neighbouring muscles to be large, the fascia over them to be thin, and — overwhelmingly — subcutaneous fat to be low.

### The regions that decide each view

| View | What a trained eye checks first | Common empty patch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Front relaxed | Neck and upper trap mass; lateral delt width; clavicular pec; forearm; outer quad sweep | Neck; brachioradialis; medial quad above the knee |
| Front flexed | Pec separation at the sternum; biceps peak and brachialis bulge; rectus abdominis blocks; adductor mass | Adductors; lower pec border |
| Side | Chest depth; delt-to-triceps step; abdominal wall flatness; glute projection; gastroc belly height | Rear delt; glute; soleus below the gastroc |
| Back relaxed | Lat flare origin point; mid-trap and rhomboid mass; erector columns; hamstring thickness | Lower trap; teres and infraspinatus region |
| Back flexed | Scapular-region detail; lat insertion low on the waist; glute-hamstring tie-in | Infraspinatus; lower lat; hamstring at the knee |

### The structural limits worth naming now

Four things are not trainable and should not be chased. Clavicle length sets shoulder width independent of muscle. Lat insertion height decides whether your lats flare from the armpit or from the mid-back. Muscle belly length and tendon length set biceps peak and gastrocnemius belly height. Rib cage width and the number of visible abdominal rows are fixed.

You can add tissue to every one of those regions. You cannot change where it attaches. Design the program around the first fact and ignore the second.

## Muscle-by-muscle audit

Every region you listed, with a verdict on whether it earns a dedicated exercise. "Direct" means at least one movement exists in the week whose primary job is that region.

### Chest

The pec is one muscle with regional fibre populations, not three muscles. Clavicular fibres run more vertically and have a shoulder-flexion bias; sternocostal fibres run horizontally; the abdominal head runs upward from the rectus sheath. Regional hypertrophy within the pec is one of the better-supported regional findings — incline pressing produces more clavicular growth than flat, and flat produces more sternal growth than incline.

- **Clavicular/upper pec** — direct, and it gets the priority slot. This is the region that decides whether the chest looks like it belongs to the shoulders or sits below them. Incline 30–35°, not 45°, because past about 40° the front delt takes over.
- **Sternal/mid pec** — direct. Flat pressing plus a horizontal fly.
- **Lower/costal pec** — direct but cheap. One dip or decline-path movement covers it. It is also the region most people already have from flat pressing.
- **Thickness and separation** — not a trainable target of its own. Sternal separation comes from mass either side of the midline plus leanness. Nothing "carves the inner chest"; adduction past the midline is a convention, not a finding.

### Shoulders

- **Anterior delt** — direct, but modestly. You asked me not to reflexively say pressing is enough, so here is the actual reasoning. The front delt is heavily loaded by every horizontal and incline press in the program, and chronically overbuilt relative to the rear in most trainees. However, the *overhead* press loads it at a longer muscle length and through a range that horizontal pressing never reaches, which is a genuinely different stimulus. Verdict: one dedicated overhead press per week in Push B, no isolation front raises. Front raises would add joint stress, eat recovery that lateral delt work needs, and push anterior-posterior balance the wrong way.
- **Lateral delt** — direct and high volume. Its contribution to outline is larger than any other single muscle in the upper body, it is barely trained by pressing (the mid-delt acts mostly as a stabiliser in a press), it recovers fast, and it tolerates frequency. This is the one place where high volume is justified rather than fashionable. Four exposures a week.
- **Posterior delt** — direct and high volume, for a different reason: it is the most commonly underdeveloped muscle in the entire physique, it is the main determinant of the side-view step from delt to arm, and rows train it only when the elbow travels high and wide. Three exposures a week.

### Back

You asked which rowing variables actually change emphasis. Three do, and the rest is convention.

1. **Elbow path relative to the torso.** Elbow tucked near the ribs with the humerus travelling behind the body = lat-biased, because the lat's dominant action is shoulder extension. Elbow flared to roughly 60–90° from the torso = mid-trap, rhomboid and rear-delt biased, because the humerus is moving in horizontal abduction. This is the single most important row variable and it is far more decisive than grip.
2. **Angle of pull relative to the torso.** A pull perpendicular to the spine loads scapular retractors; a pull along the line of the torso loads the lats. This is why a chest-supported row and a seated cable row with a leaned-back torso are not the same exercise.
3. **Scapular permission.** Allowing full protraction at the stretch and full retraction at the finish recruits rhomboids and mid-traps substantially more than locking the scapula and moving at the elbow only.

Grip (pronated vs neutral vs supinated) mostly changes elbow comfort and biceps contribution, not lat activation. Treat grip claims as convention unless someone shows you regional MRI data.

- **Latissimus dorsi** — direct, both functions. Vertical pulling (adduction) for the upper lat flare and width, and shoulder-extension work (straight-arm pulldown, low-elbow rows) for the lower lat and its insertion down the waist. Running only pulldowns builds the top of the lat and leaves the lower portion thin, which is exactly the fault you flagged.
- **Teres major** — indirect only, deliberately. It shares adduction and extension with the lat and has no access route a lat exercise doesn't already use. It will grow from the pulldown and pullover work.
- **Infraspinatus and teres minor** — direct, in small doses. This is both an appearance region on the upper back and the primary external rotator. Covered by external rotation prehab plus high-elbow rowing.
- **Rhomboids and mid-trap** — direct. High-elbow chest-supported rowing is the main driver. These are the depth-axis muscles that stop the upper back looking flat from the side.
- **Upper trap** — direct, and more than most programs give it. It is a large visual mass from the front, it is far from maximally trained by shrugging alone, and it responds to loaded carries and heavy partial shrugs. It also matters for your neck goal.
- **Lower trap** — direct but cheap. Two sets a week of a prone or cable Y-raise. Its main value is scapular upward rotation for shoulder health, with a modest appearance contribution between the shoulder blades.
- **Spinal erectors** — direct, at moderate load. I've removed heavy deadlifting for reflux reasons, so erectors need a replacement exposure, which the 45° back extension provides at a fraction of the bracing demand.
- **Serratus anterior** — direct but incidental. Covered by overhead pressing and cable pullovers. It is genuinely visible when lean and genuinely cannot be trained hard in isolation without silly movements.

### Neck

Worth training, and most lifters' single largest return on four sets a week. A developed neck changes how large you look in clothing more than any arm measurement does.

- **Sternocleidomastoid** — direct via cervical flexion. This is the visible strap at the front and side of the neck.
- **Upper trap** — covered by shrugs and carries, as above. It supplies the posterior-lateral neck mass.
- **Cervical extensors (splenius, semispinalis)** — direct via neck extension, conservatively loaded.
- **Lateral cervical musculature (scalenes, SCM lateral function)** — direct via lateral flexion, light.
- **Deep cervical flexors (longus colli/capitis)** — isometric chin-nod holds only. They are not visible and never will be. They are included for cervical posture and tolerance, not appearance, and two short holds a week is the whole prescription.

### Arms

- **Biceps long head** — direct. It crosses the shoulder, so training it with the humerus behind the torso (incline curl) puts it at long length. That is a sound biomechanical inference and the stretch-position evidence supports loading elbow flexors at length.
- **Biceps short head** — direct, via a shoulder-flexed position (preacher or high cable curl).
- **Brachialis** — direct. It sits underneath the biceps and pushes it upward, so it contributes to arm thickness disproportionately to its visibility. Neutral and pronated grips reduce biceps leverage and raise its relative share. This is inference, not isolation.
- **Brachioradialis** — direct, via reverse and hammer curls. It is a forearm muscle that reads visually as part of the upper arm when the elbow is flexed, and it is routinely neglected.
- **Triceps long head** — direct and prioritised. It crosses the shoulder, so overhead positions place it at long length, and it is the largest of the three heads and the main contributor to arm mass from the side and rear. Overhead work is the highest-value triceps decision in the program.
- **Triceps lateral head** — direct. Loaded best with the arm at the side, which is what pushdowns and close-grip pressing do.
- **Triceps medial head** — indirect, deliberately. It is active across essentially all elbow extension and sits mostly underneath; no exercise selectively loads it and it needs no slot.

You're right that heads cannot be cleanly isolated. What can be done is change the length of the biarticular head — long head of triceps, long head of biceps — using shoulder position, which is a real and defensible lever. The uniarticular heads get whatever the movement gives them.

### Forearms

Covered in full in its own section later, with the wrist-thickness question answered properly.

- **Wrist and finger flexors** — direct. The largest trainable forearm mass.
- **Wrist extensors** — direct. Smaller, but the region that makes the outer forearm look full, and almost universally skipped.
- **Brachioradialis** — direct, as above.
- **Pronators** — indirect only. Negligible visible contribution; see the earlier disagreement.
- **Grip** — indirect via loaded carries, which double as upper-trap work.

### Abdomen and torso

- **Rectus abdominis** — direct, with load and progression. You are right that low body fat alone gives you flat skin over nothing. Trained abs are visibly thicker and the intersections sit deeper.
- **Obliques** — direct but moderate, and this needs a straight answer. The fear of a thickened waist from oblique training is mostly overstated at natural training volumes; the dominant waist-width variables are skeletal structure, body fat and gut distension. That said, heavy loaded side bends and heavy weighted rotation have the largest hypertrophy potential and the least appearance payoff, so they're out. Anti-rotation and anti-lateral-flexion work at moderate load is in.
- **Transverse abdominis** — no direct work, for the reasons in the disagreement section.
- **Serratus anterior** — as under Back.

### Glutes and hips

- **Gluteus maximus** — direct. It is a depth-axis muscle, it is the largest muscle in the body, and a program that trains it only through squat patterns under-loads it at the shortened end.
- **Gluteus medius** — direct. It is the muscle that fills the gap between iliac crest and greater trochanter; an unfilled gap there makes the hips look narrow and the waist look wider by contrast.
- **Gluteus minimus** — indirect only. No access route distinct from medius work.
- **Hip abductors generally and TFL** — covered by abduction work. TFL gets no dedicated work; it is small, it is often already overactive, and nobody has ever improved a physique by growing it.
- **Adductor magnus, longus, brevis** — direct. Adductor magnus in particular is a powerful hip extensor and a large mass; it is trained heavily by wide-stance and deep hip-flexion work, and it is one of the two or three most commonly empty regions on an otherwise developed lower body.

### Quadriceps

- **Rectus femoris** — direct, and this one needs its own exercise. It crosses the hip, so it is shortened and poorly stimulated during squat patterns where the hip flexes. It is best loaded during knee extension with the hip relatively extended. Skipping this leaves a hollow down the centre of the thigh.
- **Vastus lateralis** — direct. The outer sweep. Loaded by essentially all knee extension and squat work.
- **Vastus medialis** — direct. The teardrop above the knee. Full knee flexion depth matters here more than foot position; "turn the toes out to hit the VMO" is convention.
- **Vastus intermedius** — indirect only, as discussed.

### Hamstrings

Both functions are needed and they are not interchangeable. Knee flexion and hip extension produce different regional growth, and evidence here is reasonably good: leg curls grow the distal hamstring and the short head of biceps femoris (which only crosses the knee), while hip-extension work favours the proximal and medial hamstring.

- **Biceps femoris long head** — direct, both functions.
- **Biceps femoris short head** — direct, knee flexion only. It does not cross the hip, so RDL-type work cannot train it at all. This is the single strongest argument for keeping leg curls even in a program heavy on hip hinging.
- **Semitendinosus and semimembranosus** — direct, both functions. The medial hamstring is better served by hip extension than most programs assume.

### Lower legs

- **Gastrocnemius, medial and lateral heads** — direct, trained with the knee extended. Medial head is the larger visual contributor. Foot position changes which head takes slightly more load; the effect is real but small, so I rotate it rather than building a program around it.
- **Soleus** — direct, and it needs its own exercise with the knee bent, because the gastrocnemius goes slack in that position. Soleus is the deep muscle that pushes the gastroc outward and fills the lower calf. Training calves only standing is the most common calf error.
- **Tibialis anterior** — direct. It is the front of the shin, it is visible from the front and side, it takes four sets a week, and it also supports ankle dorsiflexion, which you listed as a mobility goal.

## Forgotten muscles, and the philosophy that follows

Nine regions are missing from almost every physique-focused program, and seven of them are cheap to fix.

| Region | Why it gets skipped | Cost to fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Neck | Not trainable with normal equipment; vague fear of injury | 4 sets/week |
| Tibialis anterior | No machine in most gyms; invisible in the mirror | 4 sets/week |
| Soleus | Assumed covered by standing calf raises, which it isn't | 6 sets/week |
| Wrist extensors | Forearm training treated as grip training | 4 sets/week |
| Adductor magnus | Associated with women's training; no mirror feedback | 6 sets/week, mostly free |
| Gluteus medius | Same reason | 4 sets/week |
| Rectus femoris | Assumed covered by squatting, which under-loads it | 4 sets/week |
| Lower lat / shoulder extension | Pulldown-dominant back training | Free — a path change |
| Rear delt | Assumed covered by rowing, which only covers it at high elbow | 8 sets/week |

Two more are skipped and should stay skipped: transverse abdominis and pronator teres. They are on the list above's opposite side.

### Your training philosophy, in five rules

**1. Coverage before volume.** A region at zero sets is a bigger problem than a region at eight instead of sixteen. Every slot in this program is justified by a region, not by a tradition. Nothing is in it so I can say a muscle was "hit".

**2. Volume is allocated by return, not evenly.** Lats, lateral delts, rear delts, quads, hamstrings and chest carry the silhouette and get 14–20 direct sets a week. Lower trap, tibialis, deep neck flexors, serratus and wrist extensors get 4–6 and look finished on it. Treating these as equal is the single most common way a six-day program becomes a four-day program's results at six days' cost.

**3. One movement per job, not one movement per muscle name.** Where two exercises load the same muscle at the same length through the same path, the second is redundant regardless of what it's called. Where one muscle has two genuinely different functions — hamstrings, lats, triceps long head — it gets two.

**4. Load the stretch, control the eccentric, stop short of failure on compounds.** Train most working sets at 1–3 reps in reserve, take isolation work closer to 0–1, and reserve true failure for single-joint machine work where the failure point is safe and the recovery cost is low. The evidence that proximity to failure matters is good; the evidence that reaching failure is required is not.

**5. The hernia constraint is a selection constraint, not an intensity constraint.** Nothing in this program is easy. What changes is that spinal-compression-plus-maximal-Valsalva movements are replaced by supported and machine variants that reach the same muscle at the same effort with a fraction of the intra-abdominal pressure. You lose almost no hypertrophy stimulus. You lose the barbell ego lifts, which were never the point.

## Week structure and how to read the prescriptions

Six training days, one full rest day, with the two Push days separated by 72 hours so shoulder and triceps tissue isn't asked to grow on a 48-hour cycle twice.

| Day | Session | Emphasis | Hard sets | Target time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mon | Push A | Chest-led; incline priority; triceps long head | 20 | 80 min |
| Tue | Pull A | Lat width + rear delt + biceps; neck | 21 | 85 min |
| Wed | Legs A | Quad-led; gastrocnemius; abductors | 21 | 85 min |
| Thu | Push B | Delt-led; overhead press; lower pec; triceps lateral | 20 | 80 min |
| Fri | Pull B | Back thickness + traps + brachialis/forearm; neck | 22 | 85 min |
| Sat | Legs B | Hamstring, glute, adductor; soleus; tibialis | 21 | 85 min |
| Sun | Rest | Mobility routine only | — | 15 min |

If you can only train five days, drop Push B and move its overhead press into Push A. Don't drop a Legs day; the lower body is where the volume is already tightest.

### A/B is differentiation, not variety

A and B are not two versions of the same session. Each exposure has a different job, which is why the same muscle appears twice a week without the second exposure being a repeat. Chest gets incline-priority on Monday and a lower-pec and higher-rep exposure on Thursday. Lats get a vertical-pull emphasis Tuesday and a horizontal, low-elbow emphasis Friday. Hamstrings get knee-flexion Wednesday and both functions Saturday. Rotating exercises for novelty is not part of this; the exercises stay fixed for 8 weeks.

### Notation key

Every exercise below carries the same fields in the same order, compressed so the page stays usable in a gym.

- **Line 1** — Sets × reps @ RIR, rest interval.
- **Primary** — the region this exercise exists to train.
- **Secondary** — meaningfully loaded, but not the reason it's here.
- **D/I** — whether the contribution to each listed region is Direct or Indirect, for the volume table later.
- **Why** — the job this slot does that no other slot does.
- **Setup** — grip, stance, elbow path, where relevant.
- **ROM** — range cues, especially the stretched end.
- **Breathing** — breath and bracing instruction, written with the reflux constraint in mind.
- **Ramp** — warm-up sets needed before the first working set.
- **Error** — the one technical mistake that actually ruins the exercise.

**RIR** is reps in reserve: @2 means you stop with two good reps left. If you're not sure what your real RIR is, you're probably further from failure than you think — most lifters under-estimate by 2–4 reps. Take one isolation exercise to genuine failure every few weeks purely to recalibrate.

### How ramp-up sets are decided

Five variables decide whether an exercise needs no warm-up, one feeler, or a full ramp. They apply everywhere, so you can work out the answer for any exercise I haven't listed.

1. **Absolute load on the joint.** Heavier external load means more ramp sets. A 140 kg hack squat needs three; a 12 kg lateral raise needs zero.
2. **Position in the session.** First exercise for a body part always ramps. Second and third exercises for the same body part usually need one feeler or nothing, because the tissue and the joint are already warm and the nervous system is already recruiting.
3. **Joint overlap with what came before.** If the previous exercise used the same joint through a similar range, you inherit its warm-up. An overhead press after incline pressing needs one feeler, not three ramps.
4. **Single-joint vs multi-joint.** Isolation movements have a lower coordination demand and a smaller skill component, so they need less neural ramping. They still need one light set if they load a joint at long muscle length — a lying leg curl or an incline curl earns one feeler even though it's isolation.
5. **Small-joint movements.** Wrist curls, tibialis raises, neck work and cuff work get one light set at most, and often zero if they come late. Three warm-up sets for a wrist curl is wasted time and wasted recovery.

Ramp sets are performed with crisp, controlled reps and long enough rests that they are not fatiguing. If your warm-up is making you tired, it is too heavy or too high-rep, not too long.

## Push A — chest-led

**Warm-up, 9 minutes total.**

*General (4 min).* Upright bike or rower, RPE 3–4, enough to raise core temperature and break light sweat without leg fatigue. Avoid the treadmill if you ate within 90 minutes — bouncing plus a full stomach is the most reliable reflux trigger in a gym.

*Joint-specific (5 min), one round, not to fatigue:*

1. Band pull-apart — 15 reps, light band, slow
2. Wall slide with scapular upward rotation — 10 reps
3. Thoracic extension over a bench or foam roller — 8 slow reps, exhale into each
4. Band external rotation at 0° abduction — 12/side, very light
5. Wrist flexion/extension rocks in a quadruped position — 30 seconds
6. Scapular push-up — 10 reps

That's it. No pre-exhaust, no activation circuits, nothing that fatigues a muscle you're about to train hard.

### 1. Incline dumbbell press, 30°

4 × 6–10 @ RIR 2 · rest 150–180 s

- **Primary:** clavicular pec *(D)*
- **Secondary:** sternal pec *(D)*, anterior delt *(I)*, triceps *(I)*
- **Why:** priority slot for the region that most decides whether the upper chest ties into the shoulder. Dumbbells over barbell here: better stretch at the bottom, no fixed bar path, and no load resting over your torso while you brace.
- **Setup:** bench at 30°, feet flat, slight arch at the mid-back only. Elbows roughly 45–60° from the torso, not flared to 90°. Dumbbells travel in a shallow arc, not straight up.
- **ROM:** lower until the upper arm is slightly below torso level and you feel the pec lengthen; stop short of locking out so tension stays on the chest.
- **Breathing:** inhale on the descent, exhale through the sticking point on the way up. Do not hold your breath at the bottom. No belt.
- **Ramp:** full ramp — first pressing movement of the week. For a 40 kg/hand working weight: 40% × 8, 60% × 5, 80% × 3, then working sets.
- **Error:** setting the bench at 45°, which converts this into a front-delt press.

### 2. Flat machine chest press (converging or Hammer-style)

3 × 8–12 @ RIR 1 · rest 120 s

- **Primary:** sternal pec *(D)*
- **Secondary:** anterior delt *(I)*, triceps *(I)*
- **Why:** mid-pec at a horizontal path, with the machine removing stabilisation so you can push closer to failure safely. A machine also lets you train hard without a loaded bar above a supine torso, which matters here.
- **Setup:** handles at roughly nipple height, seat set so the stretch is felt without the shoulder rolling forward. Neutral or slightly pronated grip, whichever lets the elbow sit at 45–60°.
- **ROM:** full stretch under control, short of lockout.
- **Breathing:** exhale on the press. Shoulders and ribcage stay down; don't shrug at the top.
- **Ramp:** one feeler set at \~60% × 6. Chest and shoulders are already warm.
- **Error:** chasing load until the shoulders roll forward at the stretch.

### 3. Cable fly, low-to-high

3 × 12–15 @ RIR 1 · rest 90 s

- **Primary:** clavicular pec *(D)*
- **Secondary:** sternal pec *(D)*
- **Why:** loads the pec at long length in a path that pressing cannot reach, and the cable keeps tension at the stretched position where free-weight flies lose it.
- **Setup:** cables from the bottom pulley, slight forward lean, soft elbow held at a fixed angle throughout. Hands finish at upper-chest height, not above the head.
- **ROM:** let the arms travel back until you feel a strong pec stretch. Stop the concentric when the hands approach each other — crossing them over adds nothing.
- **Breathing:** exhale as the hands come together, normal breathing throughout. No bracing required.
- **Ramp:** none. One light practice set only if you're changing the pulley height from last week.
- **Error:** turning it into a press by bending and extending the elbow.

### 4. Seated dumbbell shoulder press

3 × 8–12 @ RIR 2 · rest 120 s

- **Primary:** anterior delt *(D)*
- **Secondary:** lateral delt *(I)*, triceps long head *(I)*, serratus anterior *(I)*
- **Why:** the only slot in the week that loads the front delt through overhead flexion. It also supplies serratus work via scapular upward rotation, which is why there's no dedicated serratus exercise.
- **Setup:** backrest at 80–85°, not vertical. Dumbbells start at ear height, elbows slightly forward of the frontal plane rather than straight out to the sides.
- **ROM:** down to ear level, up to just short of lockout.
- **Breathing:** exhale on the way up. Keep ribs down; do not arch the lower back to press more weight — that's both a lumbar and an intra-abdominal pressure problem.
- **Ramp:** one feeler at \~60% × 6. The shoulder is warm from two pressing movements.
- **Error:** letting it become an incline press by leaning the backrest too far back.

### 5. Cable lateral raise, single arm

4 × 10–15 @ RIR 0–1 · rest 75 s

- **Primary:** lateral delt *(D)*
- **Secondary:** none worth counting
- **Why:** lateral delt is the highest-return outline muscle and this is its first of four weekly exposures. Single-arm cable keeps resistance on at the bottom, where dumbbells have none, and that bottom range is where the muscle is longest.
- **Setup:** pulley at the lowest setting, cable crossing in front of the body, hand starting at the opposite hip. Lean away slightly from the stack. Lead with the elbow.
- **ROM:** raise to roughly shoulder height; higher recruits upper trap without adding delt tension.
- **Breathing:** normal. No bracing. This is a muscle you can train hard with no abdominal cost at all.
- **Ramp:** none.
- **Error:** using momentum from the hips. If the torso moves, the weight is too heavy and the delt is getting less, not more.

### 6. Overhead cable triceps extension, rope

3 × 10–12 @ RIR 1 · rest 90 s

- **Primary:** triceps long head *(D)*
- **Secondary:** triceps lateral and medial heads *(I)*
- **Why:** the long head is the largest triceps head and it is only at long length when the shoulder is flexed overhead. This is the highest-value triceps exercise in the program and it leads the triceps work for that reason.
- **Setup:** rope at high pulley, face away, split stance, elbows held close to the head and pointing forward. Torso leans forward slightly so the shoulder is in roughly 160–170° of flexion.
- **ROM:** deep stretch at the bottom with the hands behind the head; full extension at the top without locking out hard.
- **Breathing:** exhale on extension. Normal, relaxed breathing — the split stance means no bracing demand.
- **Ramp:** one light set × 10 to find the elbow position.
- **Error:** letting the elbows drift outward and forward, which shortens the long head and turns it into a pushdown.

### 7. Triceps pushdown, straight bar

3 × 12–15 @ RIR 0–1 · rest 75 s

- **Primary:** triceps lateral head *(D)*
- **Secondary:** medial head *(D)*, long head *(I)*
- **Why:** loads the triceps with the arm at the side, which is the shortened-to-mid range the overhead work doesn't cover. Second exercise, so it runs to near failure.
- **Setup:** elbows pinned to the ribs, torso upright with a very slight forward lean, pronated grip.
- **ROM:** full extension, controlled return to around 90° of elbow flexion.
- **Breathing:** normal.
- **Ramp:** none. The triceps have already done three sets.
- **Error:** leaning over the bar and using bodyweight, which turns this into a pressing movement.

### 8. Cable crunch, kneeling

3 × 12–15 @ RIR 1 · rest 75 s

- **Primary:** rectus abdominis *(D)*
- **Secondary:** obliques *(I)*
- **Why:** loaded, progressible spinal flexion — the only kind that makes abs visibly thicker rather than just present.
- **Setup:** kneeling, rope behind the head, hips fixed. The movement is spinal flexion only: ribs travel toward the pelvis.
- **ROM:** full flexion to a hard contraction, controlled return to a slight extension — not a deep backbend.
- **Breathing:** **exhale hard throughout the crunch**, inhale on the return. Never hold your breath against a flexed trunk. Moderate load, 12–15 reps — this is a deliberate reflux modification, explained later.
- **Ramp:** none.
- **Error:** pulling with the arms and hinging at the hip instead of flexing the spine.

**Session total:** 20 hard sets. Chest 10 direct, lateral delt 4 direct, front delt 3 direct, triceps 6 direct, abs 3 direct.

## Pull A — lat width, rear delt, biceps

**Warm-up, 9 minutes total.**

*General (4 min).* Rower preferred here, RPE 3–4 — it pre-warms the lats, scapular muscles and elbows in the pattern you're about to train.

*Joint-specific (5 min), one round:*

1. Dead hang from a bar — 20–30 s, relaxed, letting the lats and scapulae decompress
2. Scapular pull-up (hang, depress only) — 8 reps
3. Band pull-apart — 15 reps
4. Band external rotation at 90° abduction — 12/side, light
5. Thoracic rotation, open-book or quadruped — 8/side
6. Wrist flexor and extensor rocks — 30 s

---

### 1. Neutral-grip lat pulldown

4 × 8–12 @ RIR 2 · rest 150 s

- **Primary:** latissimus dorsi, adduction function *(D)*
- **Secondary:** teres major *(D)*, biceps *(I)*, lower trap *(I)*, rhomboids *(I)*
- **Why:** the width slot. Vertical pulling builds the upper lat and the flare point; the neutral grip is chosen for elbow and shoulder comfort across a 16-week block, not because it changes lat recruitment.
- **Setup:** thighs locked under the pad, torso leaned back 10–15° and *held there* — not rocking. Grip just outside shoulder width. Elbows drive down and slightly forward.
- **ROM:** let the scapula rise fully at the top for a genuine stretch; pull until the bar reaches the upper chest. Do not pull behind the neck.
- **Breathing:** exhale on the pull. Light trunk tension only, no breath-holding.
- **Ramp:** full ramp as the first pull of the day. For 80 kg working: 40% × 8, 60% × 5, 80% × 3.
- **Error:** rocking the torso to generate the pull, which converts it to a row and removes the stretch.

### 2. Chest-supported row, high elbow

4 × 8–12 @ RIR 1–2 · rest 150 s

- **Primary:** mid-trap *(D)*, rhomboids *(D)*, posterior delt *(D)*
- **Secondary:** lat *(I)*, biceps *(I)*, infraspinatus *(I)*
- **Why:** the back-thickness slot, and the reason this program isn't pulldown-dominated. Chest support removes the spinal-erector and bracing demand entirely, which is why it replaces barbell rowing here.
- **Setup:** chest firmly on the pad, elbows flared to roughly 60–75° from the torso, pronated or wide neutral grip. The humerus moves in horizontal abduction, not along the ribs.
- **ROM:** allow the scapulae to protract fully at the stretch and retract hard at the finish. The scapular movement is the point.
- **Breathing:** exhale on the pull. The chest pad means no trunk bracing is required at all — a significant advantage for you.
- **Ramp:** one ramp set at \~65% × 5. Back is warm from pulldowns but this is a different pattern.
- **Error:** keeping the elbows tucked, which turns a mid-back exercise into a second lat exercise and leaves the rhomboids untrained.

### 3. Straight-arm cable pulldown

3 × 12–15 @ RIR 1 · rest 90 s

- **Primary:** latissimus dorsi, shoulder-extension function *(D)*
- **Secondary:** teres major *(D)*, long head triceps *(I)*, serratus anterior *(I)*
- **Why:** this is the lower-lat and lat-insertion slot you were right to ask for. Shoulder extension with a straight arm loads the lat through a path no pulldown or row reaches, and it is the difference between lats that stop at the mid-back and lats that run down toward the waist.
- **Setup:** high pulley, straight bar or rope, hinge forward at the hips to roughly 30°, arms nearly straight with a fixed slight elbow bend.
- **ROM:** start with arms overhead and the lat fully lengthened; finish with hands at the thighs. The last 20° is where the lat is shortest and matters most.
- **Breathing:** normal. Hinged position, moderate load, no bracing.
- **Ramp:** one light set × 12 to set the hinge angle.
- **Error:** bending and extending the elbows, which hands the work to the triceps.

### 4. Reverse pec deck or cable reverse fly

4 × 12–20 @ RIR 0–1 · rest 75 s

- **Primary:** posterior delt *(D)*
- **Secondary:** infraspinatus *(D)*, mid-trap *(I)*
- **Why:** rear delt is the most under-built muscle in most physiques and rowing only reaches it at a high elbow. Four sets here plus the rowing exposure is deliberate over-weighting of a region you'll otherwise miss.
- **Setup:** elbows nearly straight or at a fixed slight bend, arms at shoulder height, moving in a pure horizontal arc. Chest against the pad.
- **ROM:** full stretch across the body, finish just past the frontal plane. Don't force a huge range at the back — there's little rear delt tension there.
- **Breathing:** normal.
- **Ramp:** none.
- **Error:** using so much load that the elbows bend and the mid-back takes over.

### 5. Incline dumbbell curl, 60° bench

3 × 8–12 @ RIR 1 · rest 90 s

- **Primary:** biceps long head *(D)*
- **Secondary:** brachialis *(I)*, biceps short head *(D)*
- **Why:** the shoulder sits behind the torso, which puts the long head at its longest. Training a muscle at long length is one of the better-supported hypertrophy levers available.
- **Setup:** bench at 60°, upper arms hanging behind the torso and staying there. Supinated grip throughout.
- **ROM:** full extension at the bottom — do not shorten the range to avoid the stretch; that *is* the exercise. Stop the concentric before the elbow passes vertical.
- **Breathing:** normal.
- **Ramp:** one light set × 8. The stretched position at the bottom earns a feeler even though it's isolation.
- **Error:** letting the elbow drift forward at the bottom, which removes the long-head stretch.

### 6. Cable curl, high pulley or preacher curl

3 × 10–12 @ RIR 0–1 · rest 75 s

- **Primary:** biceps short head *(D)*
- **Secondary:** brachialis *(I)*
- **Why:** shoulder flexed, which shortens the long head and shifts the share toward the short head. Complementary to the incline curl rather than a repeat of it.
- **Setup:** preacher pad or a cable from above; upper arm in front of the torso and fixed.
- **ROM:** full extension at the bottom, controlled.
- **Breathing:** normal.
- **Ramp:** none.
- **Error:** bouncing out of the bottom, which is where the elbow is most vulnerable in this position.

### 7. Neck flexion, plate or harness

2 × 12–15 @ RIR 3 · rest 60 s

- **Primary:** sternocleidomastoid *(D)*
- **Secondary:** deep cervical flexors *(I)*
- **Why:** first of two weekly neck exposures. Full details in the neck section — start with no load at all for the first two weeks.
- **Setup:** lying supine on a bench, head off the end, a folded towel then a light plate on the forehead.
- **ROM:** chin tucks toward the chest, smooth and slow, 2 seconds up and 3 down. Never a fast snap.
- **Breathing:** normal, never held.
- **Ramp:** one unloaded set × 10.
- **Error:** jutting the chin forward instead of tucking it.

### 8. Neck extension, harness or manual

2 × 12–15 @ RIR 3 · rest 60 s

- **Primary:** cervical extensors *(D)*
- **Secondary:** upper trap *(I)*
- **Setup:** prone on a bench, head off the end, light plate held on the back of the head.
- **ROM:** controlled nod down and up through a comfortable range only. Do not extend into end-range.
- **Breathing:** normal.
- **Ramp:** none — already warm from flexion work.
- **Error:** going heavy. Progress by reps and tempo for the first eight weeks.

### 9. Barbell or dumbbell wrist curl

3 × 15–20 @ RIR 1 · rest 60 s

- **Primary:** wrist and finger flexors *(D)*
- **Why:** the largest trainable forearm mass, and the main driver of forearm thickness.
- **Setup:** forearms on a bench or thighs, wrists over the edge, palms up. Let the bar roll to the fingertips at the bottom.
- **ROM:** full — finger extension at the bottom, full wrist flexion at the top.
- **Breathing:** normal.
- **Ramp:** none.
- **Error:** short, choppy reps. The finger-roll portion is where most of the range lives.

**Session total:** 21 hard sets. Lats 7 direct, mid-back 4 direct, rear delt 4 direct, biceps 6 direct, neck 4, forearms 3.

## Legs A — quad-led

**Warm-up, 11 minutes total.** Legs days get the longest warm-up because the joints involved have the most range to prepare and the loads are highest.

*General (5 min).* Bike, RPE 3–5, building. Enough to feel warm in the quads and hips.

*Joint-specific (6 min), one round:*

1. Ankle dorsiflexion rock against a wall — 10/side, knee tracking over the toes
2. 90/90 hip rotation transitions — 8/side
3. Bodyweight deep squat hold — 30 s, heels down, hands pushing knees out
4. Leg swings, front-to-back then lateral — 10 each direction per leg
5. Bodyweight Romanian hinge — 10 reps, feeling the hamstrings load
6. Standing calf and soleus bounces — 20 reps

---

### 1. Hack squat or pendulum squat

4 × 6–10 @ RIR 2 · rest 180 s

- **Primary:** vastus lateralis *(D)*, vastus medialis *(D)*, vastus intermedius *(I)*
- **Secondary:** gluteus maximus *(D)*, adductor magnus *(I)*, rectus femoris *(I)*
- **Why:** the heavy knee-extension-dominant slot. **This replaces the barbell back squat** — it reaches the quads at least as well, the back pad carries the axial load instead of your spine, and you can train it hard without a maximal Valsalva. If your gym has only a Smith machine, a Smith front-foot-elevated squat is the substitute.
- **Setup:** feet low and roughly shoulder-width on the platform, which increases knee travel and quad demand. Toes slightly out. Back flat against the pad.
- **ROM:** descend until the hip is below the knee if your ankles and hips allow it without the pelvis tucking under. Depth drives vastus medialis and adductor involvement.
- **Breathing:** **inhale at the top, exhale steadily through the ascent.** Do not hold a maximal breath through the whole rep. A light brace is fine; a maximal one is not. No belt. This is the most important breathing instruction in the program.
- **Ramp:** full ramp. For 140 kg working: empty sled × 10, 40% × 8, 60% × 5, 80% × 3.
- **Error:** cutting depth to add load. Half-range hack squats train a narrow slice of the quad and nothing else.

### 2. Leg press, moderate load, high rep

3 × 12–15 @ RIR 1 · rest 120 s

- **Primary:** vastus lateralis *(D)*, vastus medialis *(D)*
- **Secondary:** gluteus maximus *(D)*, adductor magnus *(D)*
- **Why:** a second knee-extension-dominant exposure at a different joint angle and higher rep range. **Deliberately kept at 12–15 reps rather than heavy** — heavy leg pressing with the hips and knees compressed into the torso is one of the highest intra-abdominal pressure positions in a gym.
- **Setup:** feet mid-platform, shoulder width or slightly wider. Hips stay down in the seat.
- **ROM:** descend until just before the pelvis starts to lift off the pad. That point is your depth limit — going past it rounds the lumbar spine and spikes abdominal pressure.
- **Breathing:** exhale on the press, inhale on the descent. No breath-holding. Do not pull on the handles to brace hard.
- **Ramp:** one ramp at \~60% × 8. Quads are warm from hack squats.
- **Error:** letting the hips curl up off the seat at the bottom.

### 3. Leg extension, torso reclined

3 × 12–15 @ RIR 0–1 · rest 90 s

- **Primary:** rectus femoris *(D)*, vastus lateralis *(D)*, vastus medialis *(D)*
- **Why:** the rectus femoris slot. Reclining the backrest extends the hip, which lengthens rectus femoris and raises its contribution — the region squats and presses systematically under-load. This is the answer to a hollow centre-thigh.
- **Setup:** backrest reclined as far as the machine allows while keeping the hips seated. Shin pad low on the ankle.
- **ROM:** full extension at the top with a one-second hold; controlled return to deep flexion.
- **Breathing:** normal. No bracing.
- **Ramp:** none.
- **Error:** sitting bolt upright, which flexes the hip and takes the rectus femoris out of the exercise.

### 4. Seated leg curl

3 × 10–12 @ RIR 1 · rest 90 s

- **Primary:** biceps femoris long head *(D)*, short head *(D)*, semitendinosus *(D)*, semimembranosus *(D)*
- **Why:** the hamstring's knee-flexion function, and the seated version holds the hip flexed so the hamstring is at long length throughout. Seated curls appear to outperform lying curls for hamstring hypertrophy, which is why the seated version is the higher-volume one in the week.
- **Setup:** hip pad tight, torso upright or slightly forward, knees aligned with the machine axis.
- **ROM:** full extension at the top, full flexion at the bottom.
- **Breathing:** normal.
- **Ramp:** one light set × 10.
- **Error:** letting the hips slide forward, which shortens the hamstring and reduces range.

### 5. Walking lunge or Bulgarian split squat

3 × 10–12 per leg @ RIR 2 · rest 120 s

- **Primary:** gluteus maximus *(D)*, adductor magnus *(D)*
- **Secondary:** quadriceps *(D)*, gluteus medius *(I)*, hamstrings *(I)*
- **Why:** this is the adductor magnus slot as much as the glute slot. A long stride with deep hip flexion loads adductor magnus heavily as a hip extensor, and unilateral work demands frontal-plane stability from gluteus medius. Dumbbells at the sides rather than a loaded barbell on the back.
- **Setup:** dumbbells held at the sides. Long stride. Torso upright for more quad, leaned slightly forward for more glute and adductor — use the forward lean here.
- **ROM:** rear knee close to the floor, deep hip flexion on the front leg.
- **Breathing:** exhale on the drive up. No bracing beyond light core tension — dumbbells at the sides keep the load off the trunk entirely.
- **Ramp:** one bodyweight set × 6 per leg.
- **Error:** short strides, which turn it into a knee-dominant movement and lose the adductor stimulus.

### 6. Standing calf raise

4 × 8–12 @ RIR 0–1 · rest 90 s

- **Primary:** gastrocnemius medial head *(D)*, lateral head *(D)*
- **Secondary:** soleus *(I)*
- **Why:** knee extended, so the gastrocnemius is at a length where it can actually produce force. Calves tolerate and need heavier loading than they usually get.
- **Setup:** toes forward for the first two sets, slightly turned out for the last two — a minor bias shift, treated as inference rather than fact. Knees straight but not locked hard.
- **ROM:** full stretch at the bottom with a 2-second pause, full plantarflexion at the top with a 1-second pause. The pause at the bottom is the whole game; bouncing uses tendon elasticity instead of muscle.
- **Breathing:** normal.
- **Ramp:** one set × 10 at a lighter load.
- **Error:** fast partial bouncing reps.

### 7. Hip abduction machine

3 × 15–20 @ RIR 0–1 · rest 60 s

- **Primary:** gluteus medius *(D)*, gluteus minimus *(I)*
- **Secondary:** TFL *(I)*, upper gluteus maximus *(I)*
- **Why:** the only loaded frontal-plane hip work in the week. Fills the gap between the iliac crest and the hip, which makes the waist read narrower by contrast.
- **Setup:** torso leaned slightly forward to bias gluteus medius over TFL. Slow, controlled.
- **ROM:** full range both directions, controlled return — don't let the stack slam.
- **Breathing:** normal.
- **Ramp:** none.
- **Error:** leaning back and using momentum, which shifts the work to TFL.

### 8. Tibialis raise

2 × 15–20 @ RIR 1 · rest 60 s

- **Primary:** tibialis anterior *(D)*
- **Why:** front-of-shin mass, plus direct support for the ankle dorsiflexion you listed as a mobility goal. Four sets a week is the entire cost.
- **Setup:** heels on a block or the edge of a plate, back against a wall, toes free to drop. Or a tib bar if your gym has one.
- **ROM:** full dorsiflexion at the top, full controlled drop at the bottom.
- **Breathing:** normal.
- **Ramp:** none.
- **Error:** rushing. This muscle responds to time under tension, not load.

**Session total:** 21 hard sets. Quads 10 direct, hamstrings 3 direct, glutes 6 direct, adductors 3 direct, abductors 3 direct, calves 4 direct, tibialis 2 direct.

### 1. Seated overhead press, machine or Smith

4 × 6–10 @ RIR 2 · rest 180 s

- **Primary:** anterior delt *(D)*
- **Secondary:** lateral delt *(D)*, triceps long head *(I)*, serratus anterior *(D)*, upper trap *(I)*
- **Why:** the only true overhead loading in the week, led here so it gets the freshest nervous system. Machine or Smith rather than a standing barbell press — standing overhead pressing with heavy load demands exactly the maximal trunk bracing you should be avoiding.
- **Setup:** backrest at 85–90°, grip just outside shoulder width, elbows roughly 30° forward of the frontal plane.
- **ROM:** down to ear or chin height, up to just short of lockout.
- **Breathing:** exhale through the press. **Ribs stay down, no lumbar extension to cheat the press.** Light brace only.

### 2. Weighted or assisted chest dip

3 × 8–12 @ RIR 2 · rest 150 s

- **Primary:** lower/costal pec *(D)*, sternal pec *(D)*
- **Secondary:** anterior delt *(I)*, triceps *(D)*
- **Why:** the lower-pec slot, and the lower pec border is what defines the bottom edge of the chest when lean. A dip is upright, so there is no supine-with-load position and no head-down posture — it suits the reflux constraint better than a decline press does.
- **Setup:** lean the torso forward 20–30° and let the elbows travel out to roughly 45°, which biases the pec over the triceps. Parallel bars at or slightly wider than shoulder width.
- **ROM:** descend until the upper arm is roughly parallel to the floor or slightly below, as shoulder mobility allows. Don't chase extreme depth.
- **Breathing:** exhale on the ascent. **If you feel reflux in the upright hanging position, substitute a decline-path cable press from high pulleys instead.** Some people find the vertical torso with abdominal compression at the bottom uncomfortable.
- **Ramp:** one bodyweight set × 5.
- **Error:** staying bolt upright, which makes it a triceps exercise.

### 3. Incline machine or Smith press, 30°

3 × 10–12 @ RIR 1 · rest 120 s

- **Primary:** clavicular pec *(D)*
- **Secondary:** anterior delt *(I)*, triceps *(I)*
- **Why:** second weekly upper-chest exposure at a higher rep range and with the stability demand removed, so you can push nearer failure than on Monday's dumbbells.
- **Setup:** as Push A's incline — 30°, elbows at 45–60°.
- **ROM:** full stretch, short of lockout.
- **Breathing:** exhale on the press, no holding.
- **Ramp:** one feeler at \~65% × 6.
- **Error:** bouncing off the chest.

### 4. Dumbbell lateral raise

3 × 12–15 @ RIR 0–1 · rest 75 s

- **Primary:** lateral delt *(D)*
- **Secondary:** upper trap *(I)*
- **Why:** second lateral delt exposure of the week, dumbbell version for the loaded top-range the cable doesn't emphasise.
- **Setup:** slight forward torso lean, elbows soft and fixed, thumbs level with or slightly below the little finger. Do not pour-the-jug aggressively — heavy internal rotation at height is unnecessary impingement risk for no gain.
- **ROM:** to shoulder height.
- **Breathing:** normal.
- **Ramp:** none.
- **Error:** swinging. Use a weight you can stop dead at the top.

### 5. Cable lateral raise, single arm, lengthened partials

2 × 12–15 @ RIR 0 · rest 60 s

- **Primary:** lateral delt *(D)*
- **Why:** the week's fourth lateral delt exposure, run as partials in the bottom half of the range after the full-range sets. Training the stretched portion of a range appears to produce at least as much hypertrophy as full range, and these let you add quality volume with very little systemic cost.
- **Setup:** as Push A's cable raise.
- **ROM:** bottom half only — from the hand at the opposite hip to roughly 45° of abduction.
- **Breathing:** normal.
- **Ramp:** none.
- **Error:** doing them first. Partials go after full-range work, not instead of it.

### 6. Cable reverse fly or face pull

3 × 15–20 @ RIR 0–1 · rest 75 s

- **Primary:** posterior delt *(D)*
- **Secondary:** infraspinatus *(D)*, mid-trap *(I)*, lower trap *(I)*
- **Why:** rear delt's second weekly exposure, placed on a push day deliberately to offset the pressing volume and keep the anterior-to-posterior ratio sane. This is also the main rotator cuff exposure of the week, which is why there's no separate cuff prehab block.
- **Setup:** face pull version — rope at face height, pull toward the forehead with the elbows high and ending in external rotation.
- **ROM:** full protraction at the stretch, finish with hands beside the ears.
- **Breathing:** normal.
- **Ramp:** none.
- **Error:** going heavy and turning it into a high row.

### 7. Close-grip Smith or machine press

3 × 8–12 @ RIR 1 · rest 120 s

- **Primary:** triceps lateral head *(D)*, medial head *(D)*
- **Secondary:** sternal pec *(D)*, anterior delt *(I)*
- **Why:** a loaded compound triceps exposure for the two heads that respond to elbow-at-side extension. Heavier than a pushdown allows, which matters for a muscle this large.
- **Setup:** grip at shoulder width — not narrower, which only stresses the wrists. Elbows tucked to roughly 30°.
- **ROM:** bar to lower chest, full extension without a hard lockout slam.
- **Breathing:** exhale on the press.
- **Ramp:** one feeler at \~65% × 6.
- **Error:** grip so narrow the wrists are forced into extension under load.

### 8. Rope overhead extension or cross-body cable extension

2 × 12–15 @ RIR 0–1 · rest 75 s

- **Primary:** triceps long head *(D)*
- **Why:** second weekly long-head exposure at a higher rep range. Two sets, because the long head already got four sets on Monday.
- **Setup:** as Push A's overhead extension.
- **ROM:** deep stretch at the bottom.
- **Breathing:** normal.
- **Ramp:** none.
- **Error:** elbows flaring forward.

### 9. Hanging knee raise

2 × 12–15 @ RIR 1 · rest 75 s

- **Primary:** rectus abdominis, lower portion *(D)*
- **Secondary:** hip flexors *(I)*, obliques *(I)*
- **Why:** posterior pelvic tilt against gravity, which loads the lower rectus in a way the cable crunch's upper-spine flexion doesn't.

- **ROM:** knees to chest with the pelvis rolling under; controlled descent.
- **Breathing:** **exhale through the curl.** Never hold a breath here.
- **Ramp:** none.
- **Error:** swinging the legs with a static pelvis, which trains hip flexors and nothing else.

### 3. Single-arm cable or dumbbell row

- **Primary:** latissimus dorsi *(D)*
- **Secondary:** mid-trap *(I)*, rhomboids *(I)*, rear delt *(I)*
- **Why:** unilateral work allows more range at the stretch — the arm can travel further forward than a bilateral row allows — and it lets you address any left-right asymmetry directly.
- **Setup:** one hand braced on a bench or the machine, torso roughly parallel to the floor or supported. Let the shoulder blade protract fully forward at the stretch.
- **ROM:** full reach forward, pull back with the elbow travelling past the torso.
- **Breathing:** exhale on the pull. Supported position means minimal trunk demand.
- **Ramp:** none.
- **Error:** rotating the torso to add range, which looks like a bigger ROM but moves the spine instead of the scapula.

### 4. Dumbbell or cable shrug

4 × 10–15 @ RIR 0–1 · rest 90 s

- **Primary:** upper trapezius *(D)*
- **Secondary:** levator scapulae *(I)*, neck mass generally *(I)*
- **Why:** upper trap is a large visual mass from front and side and it supports your neck goal. Four sets, because this is the only direct upper-trap work in the week and most programs under-dose it badly.
- **Setup:** dumbbells at the sides, arms straight, shoulders moving straight up and very slightly back. Allow a full drop at the bottom.
- **ROM:** full elevation at the top with a 1-second hold, full depression at the bottom. Do not roll the shoulders.
- **Breathing:** exhale on elevation. Avoid holding your breath with a very heavy load — use a weight you can control for 10–15 reps rather than maximal shrugs.
- **Ramp:** one set × 10 lighter.
- **Error:** short bouncing reps with too much load.

### 5. Prone or cable Y-raise

2 × 12–15 @ RIR 1 · rest 60 s

- **Primary:** lower trapezius *(D)*
- **Secondary:** mid-trap *(I)*, posterior delt *(I)*
- **Why:** lower trap drives scapular upward rotation, which is what lets the shoulder press overhead without impingement. Two sets a week is the full dose — its appearance contribution is real but modest.
- **Setup:** prone on an incline bench or cables from low pulleys. Arms at roughly 135° from the torso, thumbs up.
- **ROM:** raise to the point where the shoulder blades rotate upward; don't force end-range.
- **Breathing:** normal.
- **Ramp:** none.
- **Error:** shrugging, which recruits upper trap instead.

### 6. 45° back extension

3 × 12–15 @ RIR 2 · rest 90 s

- **Primary:** spinal erectors *(D)*, gluteus maximus *(D)*
- **Secondary:** hamstrings, hip extension *(D)*
- **Why:** the erector slot. With conventional deadlifting removed, erectors need an exposure, and this provides it with the torso supported at the hips and the load at a fraction of a deadlift's spinal compression. Bodyweight or a light plate held at the chest — not heavy.
- **Setup:** pads at the hip crease so the hip can flex freely. Keep the spine in a neutral position and hinge at the hip, or allow a small controlled spinal flexion and extension if you prefer more erector involvement.
- **ROM:** hinge to roughly 90° of hip flexion, rise to a straight line — do not hyperextend past neutral at the top.
- **Breathing:** **exhale on the rise.** No breath-holding, no heavy plate behind the head, no belt. Keep this at 12–15 reps.
- **Ramp:** one bodyweight set × 10.
- **Error:** hyperextending at the top, which loads the lumbar facets for no benefit.

### 7. Hammer curl, cross-body or neutral

3 × 10–12 @ RIR 1 · rest 90 s

- **Primary:** brachialis *(D)*, brachioradialis *(D)*
- **Secondary:** biceps *(I)*
- **Why:** the neutral grip reduces biceps leverage and raises brachialis and brachioradialis share. Brachialis pushes the biceps upward, so it adds arm thickness beyond its own visible size; brachioradialis fills the upper forearm, which reads visually as arm.
- **Setup:** neutral grip throughout, elbow at the side and fixed. Cross-body version drives the hand toward the opposite shoulder.
- **ROM:** full extension at the bottom.
- **Breathing:** normal.
- **Ramp:** none — elbow flexors have already worked via rowing.
- **Error:** swinging the elbow forward at the top.

### 8. Reverse curl, EZ bar or cable

2 × 12–15 @ RIR 0–1 · rest 75 s

- **Primary:** brachioradialis *(D)*, wrist extensors *(D)*
- **Secondary:** brachialis *(I)*
- **Why:** the pronated grip maximally disadvantages the biceps and puts the brachioradialis in its strongest position. It also loads the wrist extensors isometrically, which complements the direct extensor work next.
- **Setup:** pronated grip, wrists held straight and firm.
- **ROM:** full extension to full flexion. Light load — this exercise embarrasses people who go heavy.
- **Breathing:** normal.
- **Ramp:** none.
- **Error:** letting the wrists collapse into extension.

### 9. Reverse wrist curl

3 × 15–20 @ RIR 1 · rest 60 s

- **Primary:** wrist extensors *(D)*
- **Why:** the forearm region essentially nobody trains, and the one that fills the outer forearm. Also directly relevant to elbow health given the curling and pulling volume in this program.
- **Setup:** forearms on a bench, palms down, wrists over the edge. Very light.
- **ROM:** full flexion to full extension, slow.
- **Breathing:** normal.
- **Ramp:** none.
- **Error:** using a load heavy enough that the forearm lifts off the bench.

### 10. Neck lateral flexion + isometric rotation hold

2 × 10/side + 2 × 15 s/side · rest 45 s

- **Primary:** lateral cervical musculature *(D)*, SCM *(D)*
- **Why:** completes the four directions of neck training across the week. Rotation is trained isometrically only — loaded cervical rotation under a plate is not worth the risk.
- **Setup:** side-lying on a bench with the head off the end for lateral flexion; hand resistance against the temple for the rotation holds.
- **ROM:** small, comfortable range. Never force end-range.
- **Breathing:** normal, never held.
- **Ramp:** none.
- **Error:** adding load before eight weeks of unloaded work.

**Session total:** 22 hard sets. Lats 10 direct, mid-back 4 direct, traps 6 direct, erectors 3 direct, elbow flexors 5 direct, forearms 5 direct, neck 4.

2. Cossack squat or lateral lunge — 8/side, for the adductors
3. 90/90 hip transitions — 8/side
4. Glute bridge — 15 reps
5. Ankle dorsiflexion rock — 10/side

### 1. Seated leg curl

4 × 8–12 @ RIR 1 · rest 150 s

- **Primary:** biceps femoris long head *(D)*, short head *(D)*, semitendinosus *(D)*, semimembranosus *(D)*
- **Secondary:** gastrocnemius *(I)*
- **Why:** hamstrings lead the session because they're the priority on this day and the knee-flexion function is the one most programs under-dose. The short head of biceps femoris doesn't cross the hip, so **no amount of RDL work can train it** — this exercise is non-negotiable.
- **Setup:** hip pad tight, torso upright. Knees on the machine axis.
- **ROM:** full knee extension at the top — the stretched position is where most of the stimulus is.
- **Breathing:** normal.
- **Ramp:** full ramp — first exercise. 40% × 10, 65% × 6, 85% × 3.
- **Error:** cutting the top of the range to use more weight.

### 2. Dumbbell Romanian deadlift, moderate load

3 × 10–12 @ RIR 2 · rest 150 s

- **Primary:** hamstrings, hip-extension function *(D)*, gluteus maximus *(D)*
- **Secondary:** adductor magnus *(D)*, erectors *(I)*
- **Why:** the hip-extension hamstring function, which produces different regional growth than knee flexion. **Deliberately dumbbells at moderate load for 10–12 reps rather than a maximal barbell RDL** — see the hernia section. You lose very little hypertrophy stimulus and a great deal of intra-abdominal pressure.
- **Setup:** dumbbells at the sides or just in front of the thighs, feet hip-width, soft knees held at a fixed angle. Push the hips back, don't bend forward.
- **ROM:** descend until you feel the hamstring reach its stretch limit — usually mid-shin — and stop there. **Do not chase floor-level range at the cost of a rounded lower back.**
- **Breathing:** **inhale at the top, exhale through the ascent.** Never hold a maximal breath through the bottom position. No belt. If this movement reliably produces reflux, substitute a seated good-morning machine or a glute-ham raise.
- **Ramp:** two ramps at 50% × 8 and 75% × 5 — the stretched loaded position earns proper preparation even at moderate weight.
- **Error:** turning it into a squat by bending the knees, or rounding the lumbar spine to reach lower.

### 3. Hip thrust machine or single-leg hip thrust

3 × 10–12 @ RIR 1 · rest 120 s

- **Primary:** gluteus maximus *(D)*
- **Secondary:** hamstrings *(I)*, adductor magnus *(I)*
- **Why:** glute max at the shortened end of its range, which the squat and lunge patterns under-load. **A machine or single-leg version rather than a heavy barbell across the abdomen** — a loaded bar pressing into the abdominal wall during forceful hip extension is a poor idea with reflux, and the machine removes that entirely.
- **Setup:** upper back on the pad or bench, chin tucked, ribs down. Drive through the heels.
- **ROM:** full hip extension at the top with a 1-second squeeze; controlled descent to a deep hip flexion.
- **Breathing:** **exhale through the lockout.** Do not hold a breath at the top under load.
- **Ramp:** one set × 10 lighter.
- **Error:** hyperextending the lumbar spine at the top instead of finishing the hip.

### 4. Lying leg curl

3 × 10–12 @ RIR 0–1 · rest 90 s

- **Primary:** hamstrings, knee flexion *(D)*
- **Secondary:** gastrocnemius *(D)*
- **Why:** a different hip position from the seated curl, so the hamstring is loaded at a different point on its length-tension curve. It also loads the gastrocnemius at the knee, supplementing the calf work.
- **Setup:** prone, hips flat against the pad, knees just off the edge.
- **ROM:** full extension at the bottom, full flexion at the top.
- **Breathing:** normal. **Keep the hips down — do not let the pelvis lift**, which both reduces hamstring tension and pushes the abdomen into the pad.
- **Ramp:** none.
- **Error:** letting the hips rise to cheat the last reps.

### 5. Wide-stance leg press or adductor machine

3 × 12–15 @ RIR 1 · rest 90 s

- **Primary:** adductor magnus *(D)*, longus *(D)*, brevis *(D)*
- **Secondary:** gluteus maximus *(D)*, quadriceps *(I)*
- **Why:** the direct adductor slot. Wide stance with deep hip flexion loads adductor magnus heavily as a hip extensor; if your gym has a good adductor machine, that targets longus and brevis more directly. Rotate between them across blocks.
- **Setup:** wide-stance leg press — feet high and wide on the platform, toes turned out roughly 30°. Adductor machine — torso upright, full abduction at the stretch.
- **ROM:** deep, controlled. The stretch end is where adductors grow.
- **Breathing:** exhale on the effort, same leg-press rules as Legs A — moderate load, no breath-holding.
- **Ramp:** one set × 10 lighter.
- **Error:** shallow range on the adductor machine, which makes it nearly useless.

### 6. Seated calf raise

4 × 10–15 @ RIR 0–1 · rest 75 s

- **Primary:** soleus *(D)*
- **Why:** the knee is bent, which takes the gastrocnemius slack and leaves the soleus to do the work. **This is the exercise standing calf raises cannot replace.** Soleus is the deep muscle that pushes the gastroc outward and fills the lower calf — training calves standing only is why most calves look thin from the side.
- **Setup:** knees at roughly 90°, pad over the lower thigh, balls of the feet on the block.
- **ROM:** full stretch at the bottom with a 2-second pause, full plantarflexion at the top with a 1-second pause.
- **Breathing:** normal.
- **Ramp:** one set × 12 lighter.
- **Error:** bouncing. Soleus is slow-twitch dominant and responds to controlled tension and higher reps.

### 7. Standing calf raise

2 × 12–15 @ RIR 0–1 · rest 60 s

- **Primary:** gastrocnemius *(D)*
- **Why:** second weekly gastroc exposure at higher reps, two sets only because Wednesday already supplied four heavier ones.
- **Setup/ROM/Breathing:** as Legs A.
- **Ramp:** none.
- **Error:** as Legs A.

### 8. Tibialis raise

2 × 15–20 @ RIR 1 · rest 60 s

- **Primary:** tibialis anterior *(D)*
- **Why:** second weekly exposure, completing four sets a week.
- **Setup/ROM:** as Legs A.
- **Ramp:** none.

### 9. Pallof press or suitcase carry

3 × 12/side or 3 × 30 m per side · rest 60 s

- **Primary:** obliques *(D)*
- **Secondary:** grip *(D)*, upper trap *(D)*, erectors *(I)*
- **Why:** anti-rotation and anti-lateral-flexion loading, which trains the obliques without the heavy loaded side bends that have the most waist-thickening potential and the least appearance payoff. The carry version also doubles as the week's grip and a second upper-trap exposure.
- **Setup:** Pallof — cable at chest height, press straight out and resist rotation. Carry — one heavy dumbbell, stand tall, do not lean away.
- **ROM:** Pallof: full extension, hold 2 s. Carry: walk normally, ribs stacked over pelvis.
- **Breathing:** **breathe normally throughout — do not hold your breath during the carry.** This matters. Loaded carries tempt a sustained brace; keep breathing.
- **Ramp:** none.
- **Error:** letting the torso rotate on the Pallof, or shrugging and leaning on the carry.

## Direct neck training

Two sessions a week, four directions, and **eight weeks of unloaded work before a single plate touches your head.**

The cervical spine tolerates training well when it's progressed slowly and badly when it isn't, and there is no version of this where rushing is worth it. The neck responds quickly — this is a muscle group where most people see visible change in 10–12 weeks — so there is no reason to hurry.

| Direction | Day | Sets × reps | Intensity | Progression route |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Flexion (chin to chest) | Pull A | 2 × 12–15 | RIR 3 | Reps → tempo → load |
| Extension | Pull A | 2 × 12–15 | RIR 3 | Reps → tempo → load |
| Lateral flexion | Pull B | 2 × 10/side | RIR 3 | Reps → tempo → load |
| Rotation | Pull B | 2 × 15 s/side isometric | Submaximal | Hold duration only — never loaded |
| Deep cervical flexor hold | Pull A | 1 × 2 × 20 s | Light | Hold duration only |

### The progression, in order

**Weeks 1–2.** Everything unloaded. Flexion and extension against no resistance, lateral flexion side-lying with just the head's weight. Learn what a smooth, controlled 2-second-up, 3-second-down rep feels like. If you have any neck symptoms at all during this phase, stop and see a physio before continuing.

**Weeks 3–8.** Still no external load. Progress by adding reps (12 → 15 → 18) and then by slowing the eccentric to 4 seconds. Add the deep cervical flexor holds: lying supine, gently nod the chin toward the throat without lifting the head, hold 20 seconds. These are for cervical endurance and posture, not appearance — they will never be visible and that's fine.

**Weeks 9–16.** Introduce a 1.25 kg or 2.5 kg plate on a folded towel, for flexion and extension only. Add no more than 1.25 kg every two to three weeks. A harness is optional and a plate works fine; if you use a harness, it's for extension, not for swinging weight.

**Beyond week 16.** Most of your neck development will come from the first six months of consistent light work plus the shrug and carry volume in Pull B. Loads above about 10 kg on the forehead buy very little extra and raise risk meaningfully. There is no bodybuilding reason to go heavier.

### What never gets trained

- **Wrestler's bridge, front or back.** It loads a near-end-range cervical spine with full bodyweight. The risk is real and the hypertrophy return is no better than a plate on the forehead.
- **Loaded cervical rotation.** Rotation under external load places shear through the upper cervical segments. Isometric only.
- **Fast or ballistic neck reps in any direction.**
- **Neck training to failure.** RIR 3 is the floor for the whole first year. Unlike a bicep, there's nothing to gain from the last two reps here.
- **Neck work on a day you have a headache, neck stiffness, or any arm tingling or numbness.** Those last two mean stop training the neck and get assessed.

### Stop signals

Stop the set and the exercise for the day if you get: dizziness, visual disturbance, ringing in the ears, any tingling or numbness travelling into the arm or hand, sharp pain rather than muscular burn, or a headache that starts during the set. Any of those recurring across sessions means see a doctor before training the neck again.

## Forearm and wrist completeness

You asked what actually makes a forearm look thick near the wrist. The honest answer is that **almost nothing does, and you should stop aiming there.**

The distal third of the forearm is tendon, not muscle. The flexor and extensor muscle bellies taper into tendons roughly two-thirds of the way down, and the wrist itself is bone, retinaculum and tendon with essentially no contractile tissue. Wrist circumference is a skeletal measurement and does not change with training.

What *does* change, and what creates the impression of a thick forearm:

1. **The flexor mass on the ulnar side of the upper forearm** — the largest trainable forearm volume, built by wrist and finger flexion.
2. **The extensor mass on the radial/dorsal side** — smaller, but it fills the outer forearm and is the region nearly everyone skips.
3. **Brachioradialis** — the ridge running from the lateral elbow down the thumb side. It is the single most visually dominant forearm muscle from the front, and it's trained by neutral and pronated elbow flexion, not by wrist work.
4. **Low body fat over the forearm** — vein and tendon definition, which reads as "thick forearms" more than circumference does.

A forearm that looks thick is a forearm with a large belly high up that tapers sharply to a small wrist. The taper is the aesthetic, not a filled wrist. Chasing distal thickness is chasing an anatomy that doesn't exist.

### The prescription

| Exercise | Day | Sets × reps | Region |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wrist curl | Pull A | 3 × 15–20 | Wrist and finger flexors |
| Reverse wrist curl | Pull B | 3 × 15–20 | Wrist extensors |
| Hammer curl | Pull B | 3 × 10–12 | Brachioradialis, brachialis |
| Reverse curl | Pull B | 2 × 12–15 | Brachioradialis, extensors |
| Suitcase carry | Legs B | 3 × 30 m/side | Grip, flexors isometric |

That is 11 direct sets a week plus carries, which is more forearm work than most physique programs contain and is roughly the right dose for a muscle group with high slow-twitch content and fast recovery.

### Rules for forearm work

**Use high reps.** Forearm muscles respond poorly to heavy low-rep work and the wrist joint tolerates it badly. Nothing below 12 reps except hammer curls.

**Full range on wrist curls means the finger roll.** Let the bar roll down to the fingertips at the bottom and curl it back into the palm before flexing the wrist. Skipping this removes the finger flexors, which are a meaningful share of the mass.

**Don't train grip separately.** Dead hangs, carries and the pulling volume in this program already supply more grip work than a dedicated grip exercise would, and the carries double as trap work. A gripper adds nothing you need.

**Train extensors as seriously as flexors.** Most lifters have a 5:1 flexor-to-extensor volume ratio from pulling work alone. That imbalance contributes to lateral elbow pain, and given the curling and rowing volume in this program, the reverse wrist curls are prehab as much as hypertrophy.

**Put forearm work last.** Grip fatigue compromises rowing and pulldowns. If you ever need to move it, put it on a leg day, never before back work.

## Core and serratus

Eight direct sets a week across three days, with the load kept moderate and the breathing rules stricter than anywhere else in the program.

| Exercise | Day | Sets × reps | Region | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cable crunch | Push A | 3 × 12–15 | Rectus abdominis, upper | Loaded spinal flexion, progressible |
| Hanging knee raise | Push B | 2 × 12–15 | Rectus abdominis, lower | Posterior pelvic tilt against gravity |
| Pallof press / suitcase carry | Legs B | 3 × 12/side | Obliques | Anti-rotation and anti-lateral-flexion |
| Serratus | — | — | Serratus anterior | Covered by overhead press and straight-arm pulldown |

### Rectus abdominis

You're right that leanness alone gives you flat skin over nothing. The rectus hypertrophies like any other muscle and trained abs sit visibly higher off the abdominal wall with deeper tendinous intersections. It needs progressive load, not 200 crunches.

The constraint is that heavy loaded spinal flexion with a breath held is close to the worst combination available for reflux. So: 12–15 reps, moderate load, exhale through the concentric, no weighted sit-ups, no heavy Russian twists, no decline sit-ups with a plate. Progress by adding load slowly within that rep range, and when you can do 3 × 15 comfortably, add weight.

Upper and lower rectus are one muscle with one set of fibres, but there's reasonable evidence of regional activation differences — spinal flexion from the top (cable crunch) and pelvic tilt from the bottom (hanging knee raise) aren't identical. That's why both are here. Treat it as sound inference, not established fact.

### Obliques

The straight answer on your waist concern: **moderate oblique training will not visibly thicken your waist, and the worry has been overstated.** Waist width is driven by skeletal structure, body fat, and abdominal distension — in that order. What *can* add measurable width is years of very heavy loaded lateral flexion and heavy weighted rotation, which are exactly the two exercises with the worst return on appearance anyway.

So obliques are trained isometrically, anti-rotation and anti-lateral-flexion. You get the development, the trunk stability transfer, and none of the hypertrophy stimulus that would thicken the region. Heavy dumbbell side bends are out and stay out.

### Serratus anterior

No dedicated exercise, deliberately. Serratus is trained by scapular upward rotation and protraction, which the seated overhead press supplies through its full overhead range and the straight-arm pulldown supplies at the stretch. Both are already in the program for other reasons.

The things usually prescribed for serratus — standing cable punches, scap push-ups with load — produce little tension at a muscle that is genuinely hard to load in isolation. If your serratus doesn't show when you're lean, the cause is almost always body fat, not a missing exercise. Add two sets of weighted scapular push-ups at the end of Push B if you want insurance, but it's optional.

### Transverse abdominis

No work, as discussed. It is not visible, there's no evidence that bracing drills hypertrophy it meaningfully, and the trunk stability you need comes from the Pallof presses and carries already programmed. Drawing-in drills are a rehab intervention that migrated into bodybuilding and don't belong here.

### The breathing rule that applies to all core work

**Exhale through every rep of every abdominal exercise.** No breath-holding against a flexed or loaded trunk, ever. This is the single most consistent reflux-relevant instruction in the program, and core work is where it's easiest to forget.

## Daily mobility routine — 13 minutes

Performed in the evening or on waking, **separately from training** and at least three hours from any lifting session.

### First, the four words you asked me to distinguish

**Flexibility** is how far a joint can be moved passively — someone else or gravity moves you. **Mobility** is how far you can move it actively, under your own control. Mobility is the one that matters, because passive range you can't control is range you can't use and range that doesn't protect you under load.

**Warm-up** is raising tissue temperature and rehearsing a movement pattern immediately before training. It is not mobility work and the two have different goals.

**Rehabilitation** is the structured loading of an injured or symptomatic tissue. Nothing below is rehab. If something hurts, this routine is not the answer.

**What I won't claim:** stretching does not reliably prevent injury, and the evidence on that is genuinely mixed to negative. It does not prevent soreness. What it does reliably do is increase range of motion, mostly by increasing stretch tolerance rather than by lengthening tissue — and recent work suggests long-duration stretching can produce small hypertrophy effects, though not enough to matter against what you're doing in the gym. The honest case for this routine is: you want to retain usable range while adding mass, and consistent loaded end-range work achieves that.

**And one correction to a common belief:** gaining muscle does not make you stiff. Lifting through full range maintains or improves flexibility. People get stiff from training through partial ranges, not from being muscular.

### The routine

| # | Drill | Type | Dose | Target |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Neck: flexion/extension, rotation, lateral flexion | Dynamic | 5 slow reps each direction | Cervical range |
| 2 | Thoracic extension over a roller | Dynamic | 8 reps, exhale into each | T-spine extension |
| 3 | Open-book thoracic rotation | Dynamic | 8/side | T-spine rotation |
| 4 | Shoulder CARs (slow controlled circle) | Active | 3/side, 20 s each | Shoulder, all directions |
| 5 | Doorway or bench pec stretch | Static | 2 × 30 s/side | Pec, anterior shoulder |
| 6 | Lat stretch, hanging or on a bench | Static | 2 × 30 s/side | Lat, teres |
| 7 | Wrist flexor + extensor rocks, quadruped | Dynamic | 30 s each | Wrist, forearm |
| 8 | Couch stretch (hip flexor + rectus femoris) | Static | 2 × 45 s/side | Hip flexor, quad |
| 9 | 90/90 hip transitions | Active | 8/side | Hip internal + external rotation |
| 10 | Frog stretch or seated adductor | Static | 2 × 45 s | Adductors |
| 11 | Seated or standing hamstring stretch | Static | 2 × 30 s/side | Hamstring |
| 12 | Knee-to-wall ankle dorsiflexion | Loaded end-range | 10/side, 3 s hold | Ankle dorsiflexion |
| 13 | Straight-leg + bent-knee calf stretch | Static | 30 s each per side | Gastrocnemius, soleus |

### How to perform them

**Static holds.** 30–45 seconds, taken to a strong stretch sensation that is not painful — roughly 7/10. **Breathe slowly and continuously; never hold your breath.** Long static holds are what produce range gains, and 30 seconds is roughly where the returns start. Two sets beats one; four doesn't beat two by much.

**Dynamic and active drills.** Move slowly and under control through the full range, 5–8 repetitions. These are about controlling range, not forcing it. Shoulder CARs in particular should be slow enough to feel sticky — 20 seconds for one circle is right.

**The ankle drill is loaded, not stretched.** Knee travels over the toe with the heel flat, hold the end position for 3 seconds, repeat. Ankle dorsiflexion responds much better to loaded end-range work than to passive calf stretching, which is why it's prescribed this way.

**Frequency.** Daily is the target and five days a week is fine. Range gains from stretching regress within a few weeks of stopping, so consistency matters far more than any individual session's intensity.

**Total time:** about 13 minutes. If you only have six, keep: couch stretch, 90/90s, ankle drill, pec stretch, thoracic extension. Those five cover the ranges most threatened by this program's exercise selection.

## Pre-workout vs post-workout vs at-home stretching

The rule is simple: **dynamic before, static after or separately, never long static holds immediately before a heavy set.**

| Timing | What goes there | What never goes there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before lifting | Dynamic drills, active CARs, loaded end-range work, ramp sets | Static holds over 30 s on the muscles you're about to train |
| After lifting | Short static stretches, 20–30 s, on what you trained | Aggressive new-range work on fatigued tissue |
| Separately at home | The full 13-minute routine, long static holds, the ranges you're actively trying to improve | Nothing — this is where the real work happens |

### Why static holds don't go before heavy work

Static stretching held longer than about 60 seconds per muscle produces a short-term reduction in force output and rate of force development. The effect is real but modest, it's largest with very long holds, and it mostly disappears within 10 minutes or after a few ramp sets. Treat it as established, but not as a reason for panic.

The practical rules that follow:

- Holds under 30 seconds before training are fine and won't meaningfully cost you anything.
- If you need a longer hold to get into position for an exercise — a lat stretch before pulldowns, a pec stretch before incline — do it, then do your ramp sets. The ramp restores force output.
- Don't do a 2-minute couch stretch and then immediately hack squat. Put that at home.

### Why static holds work well after lifting

Tissue is warm, you're not about to need maximal force, and the fatigue means you're less likely to push range aggressively. 20–30 seconds on the two or three muscles you trained hardest is a reasonable five-minute addition. It will not reduce soreness — that claim has been tested repeatedly and doesn't hold up — but it is a convenient time to accumulate range work.

### Why the real work is at home

Range of motion improvements come from accumulated time at end range over weeks. Five minutes after a session, three days a week, is not enough volume to change anything. Thirteen minutes daily is. Separating it from training also means you can do it unhurried and without it competing with your warm-up or your recovery between sets.

### One thing this program already does for your mobility

Every exercise above is prescribed with a full stretched range: deep hack squats, full-extension leg curls, incline curls with the arm behind the torso, cable flies at full pec length, paused calf raises at the bottom. Loaded training through full range improves flexibility on its own, and there's reasonable evidence it does so comparably to static stretching. The 13-minute routine is insurance and targeted work on the ranges lifting doesn't reach — cervical rotation, hip internal rotation, thoracic rotation — not the main driver.

### Where the repetitive-stress risk actually sits

Three places, and I've managed each rather than ignored it.

**The shoulder, from pressing volume.** Twelve pressing sets a week across two days, all at 30° incline or overhead. The mitigations already built in: no flat barbell bench with a fixed bar path, elbows at 45–60° rather than flared, no behind-the-neck anything, seven weekly sets of external rotation work, and a 1.4:1 pull-to-push ratio. If your shoulders still get cranky by week 8, the first thing to cut is the Push B incline press, not the rowing.

**The elbow, from curling plus pulling.** Eleven direct elbow-flexion sets plus seventeen pulling sets is real cumulative tendon load. Mitigations: grip rotation across the week, reverse curls kept light, reverse wrist curls as direct extensor work, and no exercise below 8 reps for the elbow flexors. Medial or lateral elbow pain means drop the reverse curl and halve the hammer curls for two weeks, not push through.

**The knee, from quad volume.** Thirteen knee-extension sets weekly with deep hack squats. Mitigations: knee flexion volume matched at thirteen sets, which is the main structural protection the joint has; full rather than partial range, which distributes load rather than concentrating it; and no jumping or plyometric work on top. Anterior knee pain usually means the leg extension load went up too fast — it's the exercise with the highest patellofemoral compression in the program.

### What I deliberately did not include

**No band circuits before every session.** The joint-specific warm-ups contain 5–6 drills, once through, and that's enough. Longer activation circuits eat time and recovery and have no evidence base for hypertrophy or injury reduction at this volume.

**No separate rotator cuff day.** Seven weekly sets of face pulls and reverse flies, performed with deliberate external rotation, is more cuff work than most dedicated prehab programs deliver.

**No "corrective" exercise for posture.** If your thoracic position is poor, the thoracic extension work in the daily routine plus the rowing volume handles it. Postural correction exercises beyond that are a large time commitment with weak evidence.

### Three notes on the numbers

**Hamstring hip extension is the one genuinely thin spot**, and I left it there on purpose. The reason is the hernia constraint: heavy RDLs and conventional deadlifts are the standard high-volume route to that function, and both are off the table. If your reflux is well controlled by week 8, adding a second RDL exposure or a glute-ham raise on Legs A is the first volume increase I'd make.

**Anterior delt at 12 effective sets looks high for a muscle getting only 7 direct.** That's correct and intentional — the indirect load from pressing is substantial and real. **Nothing is in the 'unnecessarily excessive' column**, which is unusual for a six-day program. That's the direct result of capping sessions at 7–9 exercises and rotating small muscles rather than training everything every day.

## 12–16 week progression

The exercises do not change for 16 weeks. Everything else does.

| Block | Weeks | Sets/session | Working RIR | Focus |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 — Accumulation | 1–4 | 18–20 | 2–3 | Learn the movements, establish loads, build work capacity |
| 2 — Intensification | 5–8 | 20–22 | 1–2 | Load progression; add the first set increases |
| Deload | 9 | 12–14 | 3–4 | Same exercises, 60% of load, half the sets |
| 3 — Peak volume | 10–13 | 22–24 | 0–2 | Highest volume of the cycle; isolation to failure |
| Deload | 14 | 12–14 | 3–4 | As week 9 |
| 4 — Consolidate | 15–16 | 18–20 | 1–2 | Drop volume, hold loads, test lifts, reassess |

### Load and rep progression — double progression

Work within the prescribed rep range at a fixed load until you hit the top of the range for all working sets at the target RIR. Then add load and drop back to the bottom of the range.

Example for incline dumbbell press at 6–10 reps:

1. Week 1: 30 kg × 8, 8, 7, 6 @ RIR 2
2. Week 2: 30 kg × 9, 9, 8, 7
3. Week 3: 30 kg × 10, 10, 9, 8
4. Week 4: 30 kg × 10, 10, 10, 10 → progression triggered
5. Week 5: 32.5 kg × 7, 7, 6, 6 → cycle restarts

For isolation work where the smallest load increment is large relative to the weight — lateral raises, wrist curls, neck work — progress by adding reps well past the top of the range (up to 20 on a 12–15 exercise) before increasing load. Jumping from 10 to 12.5 kg on a lateral raise is a 25% increase; earn it with reps first.

### When to increase volume

Add sets only when **all four** of these are true:

1. You've hit the top of the rep range on that exercise for two consecutive sessions.
2. You are not sore from the previous session of that muscle by the time you train it again.
3. Your sleep and food have been consistent for at least two weeks.
4. The muscle in question is below 15 effective weekly sets.

Add **one set to one exercise at a time**, then hold for two weeks before adding another. Adding three sets across three muscles in one week makes the cause of any subsequent stall unidentifiable.

The first increases I'd make, in order: hamstring hip extension (a second RDL exposure on Legs A), lower pec (a high-to-low fly on Push A), then lats.

### When to reduce volume

Cut volume when **any** of these shows up:

- Performance drops on the same exercise for two consecutive sessions at the same bodyweight and food intake.
- A joint is achy at rest, not just during training.
- Resting heart rate is elevated 5+ bpm for several days, or sleep quality drops without another cause.
- You're avoiding a session mentally rather than just being tired.
- Soreness from a session hasn't cleared by the next exposure of that muscle, two sessions running.
- You enter a calorie deficit — cut 20% of total sets immediately, not after performance falls.

The first cut is always the last set of the last two exercises in a session, never a whole exercise. The second cut is a whole exercise from the session's tail.

### Deload specifics

Weeks 9 and 14. Keep every exercise, keep the exercise order, keep the full warm-up and the mobility routine. Halve the working sets and use roughly 60% of your usual load, stopping at RIR 4. Do not take the week off entirely and do not substitute cardio — maintaining the movement pattern is the point.

### How warm-up sets change as loads increase

Ramp sets scale with **absolute load, not percentage.** The percentages I gave are a starting template for intermediate loads. As your working weights climb:

| Working load | Ramp sets | Example for the top set |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Under 60 kg | 2 | 50% × 8, 75% × 4 |
| 60–100 kg | 3 | 40% × 8, 60% × 5, 80% × 3 |
| 100–150 kg | 3–4 | 40% × 8, 60% × 5, 75% × 3, 88% × 1 |
| Over 150 kg | 4 | 40% × 8, 55% × 5, 70% × 3, 85% × 1 |

The rep counts in the ramp **go down as the percentages go up** — never do 8 reps at 80%. The purpose of the heavier ramp sets is neural, not metabolic, so a single rep at 85% does the job and costs nothing.

Also worth knowing: ramp requirements change with training age. As you get stronger, you'll need more ramp sets for the same exercise, because the absolute load on the joint is higher. The warm-up that worked at 100 kg will be inadequate at 160 kg even though the percentages are identical.

### After week 16

Reassess with photos and the volume table, not with how you feel. Identify the two regions that lagged, reallocate 3–4 sets to each from the regions that are furthest along, swap in different exercises for the same functional slots, and run another 16 weeks. The structure stays; the emphasis moves.

## Assessing mobility progress

Retest every four weeks, in the morning, before training, after five minutes of light cardio only. Same conditions each time or the numbers mean nothing.

| Test | How to measure | Good range | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ankle dorsiflexion | Knee-to-wall: toe distance from wall with heel down, knee touching | 10–12 cm | The most useful single test here; asymmetry over 2 cm is worth addressing |
| Shoulder flexion | Lying supine, knees bent, lumbar flat: can the arms reach the floor overhead with straight elbows? | Arms flat, no rib flare | Rib flare invalidates the test |
| Shoulder external rotation | Arm at 90° abduction, measure forearm angle past vertical | 90° | Directly relevant to your pressing volume |
| Thoracic rotation | Seated, hips locked, rotate with a dowel across the chest | 45°/side | Hips must not move |
| Thoracic extension | Lying over a roller at mid-back, arms overhead: can the head reach the floor? | Head touches |  |
| Hip internal rotation | Seated, knee at 90°, rotate the lower leg outward | 35–45° | Commonly the worst score in lifters |
| Hip flexor length | Thomas test: lying at a table edge, does the thigh rest flat? | Thigh flat, knee at 80° |  |
| Hamstring | Supine straight-leg raise, opposite leg flat | 80° | Measure with a phone's inclinometer against the shin |
| Adductors | Seated straddle, measure the angle between legs | 90°+ |  |
| Cervical rotation | Seated, chin level, rotate as far as comfortable | 80°/side | Never force this one |

### How to read the results

**Progress is slow and that's normal.** Expect 2–5° per month on a restricted range, and most of it in the first 8–12 weeks. After that, gains flatten and you're maintaining.

**Asymmetry matters more than absolute numbers.** A 15% side-to-side difference on any test is worth targeted work on the restricted side — an extra set of the relevant stretch, daily. A symmetrical "below average" score on a test is often just your anatomy, particularly for hip internal rotation, where bony structure varies enormously between people and no amount of stretching changes it.

**If a test doesn't improve in eight weeks, the limit is probably structural, not muscular.** Hip internal rotation and shoulder flexion are the two most likely to be bone-limited. Stop pushing those and maintain what you have. Forcing range against a bony block is how people get joint pain.

**Active range is the real test.** If your passive hamstring raise is 85° but you can only actively lift the leg to 60°, the gap is a strength and control problem, not a flexibility problem — the answer is loaded work through that range, not more stretching.

### What should improve, and what shouldn't change

Expect improvement in: ankle dorsiflexion, thoracic extension and rotation, hip flexor length, adductor range. These respond well and the program plus routine targets them directly.

Expect little change in: hip internal rotation past your structural limit, shoulder flexion if you have a flat thoracic spine, cervical rotation. Maintain these; don't chase them.

**Watch for regression.** If ankle dorsiflexion or shoulder flexion drops by more than 10% across a month, something in the program is accumulating — usually calf volume or pressing volume outpacing the stretch work. That's a signal to add a set to the relevant stretch, not to cut the training.

## Hiatal hernia modifications

**What the evidence actually supports, stated honestly.** There is no good trial evidence that resistance training worsens a hiatal hernia, and none that any particular exercise causes one to enlarge. What is reasonably established is narrower: raised intra-abdominal pressure transiently increases reflux episodes, maximal Valsalva produces the highest intra-abdominal pressures in a gym, and exercising soon after eating provokes symptoms. Everything below follows from those three facts plus mechanism. It is risk reduction, not treatment, and your gastroenterologist outranks it.

### The six exercises you flagged, and what I did with each

| You flagged | Verdict | What's in the program instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heavy conventional deadlift | **Removed entirely** | Chest-supported T-bar row + 45° back extension |
| Maximal RDL | **Modified** | Dumbbell RDL, 10–12 reps, RIR 2, no belt |
| Heavy barbell back squat | **Removed entirely** | Hack or pendulum squat |
| Very heavy leg press | **Modified** | Leg press at 12–15 reps, moderate load |
| Aggressive loaded ab flexion | **Modified** | Cable crunch 12–15 with forced exhale; no weighted sit-ups |
| Repeated maximal Valsalva | **Removed as a technique** | Exhale-through-the-rep on every compound |

**On the deadlift specifically:** removing it costs you nothing for a physique goal. It is a hip-hinge pattern that trains erectors, glutes, hamstrings and traps — all of which are trained in this program by movements that don't require holding a maximal breath against a braced trunk while a loaded bar compresses your spine. The deadlift is a good exercise. It is not a necessary one, and for you the risk-return is poor.

**On the back squat:** same reasoning. The hack squat reaches the quadriceps at least as effectively, and the back pad takes the axial load. You lose nothing but the number on the bar.

### Breathing and bracing rules

These apply to every exercise in the program and they are the most important instruction in this document.

1. **Exhale through the concentric.** On every compound lift, breathe out steadily through the hard part of the rep rather than holding a breath. This is the single largest reduction in peak intra-abdominal pressure available to you.
2. **Inhale at the top or during the eccentric**, where the load is lowest.
3. **Brace lightly, not maximally.** You need enough trunk tension to keep the spine stable. You do not need the maximal breath-hold-and-bear-down that powerlifters use, and none of the exercises here require it because none of them load the spine axially at maximal intensity.
4. **Never hold your breath on abdominal work.** Loaded trunk flexion plus a held breath is the highest-pressure combination you can construct.
5. **No weightlifting belt.** A belt works by raising intra-abdominal pressure. For you that's the opposite of the goal, and nothing in this program needs one.
6. **If you catch yourself breath-holding, the weight is too heavy.** That's the signal, and it's a reliable one.

### Timing and food

- **Leave 2–3 hours after a substantial meal** before training. This is the single most effective modification and the easiest to skip.
- **Leave 60–90 minutes after a small snack.**
- Keep pre-workout fluid volume moderate — sip rather than drinking 750 ml immediately before.
- Avoid obvious personal triggers pre-workout: caffeine on an empty stomach, carbonated drinks, high-fat meals.
- Don't lie flat immediately after training if you've eaten.

### Positional notes

**Avoid head-down and steep decline positions under load.** That means no decline bench press, no decline sit-ups, no inverted rows with the head below the hips. Nothing in this program uses them.

**Supine loaded positions are fine**, which is why dumbbell incline pressing and lying leg curls are in. The issue is head-below-stomach, not back-on-a-bench.

**Upright is best.** It's part of why dips, cable work, machine presses and hack squats dominate this program.

### Symptoms: continue, modify, or stop

**Continue the session, note it, modify next time:** mild heartburn that resolves between sets; a single burp with a sour taste; mild upper abdominal fullness.

**Stop that exercise for the day and substitute:** reflux during a specific exercise that returns each time you perform it; a sensation of food or acid rising into the throat; chest burning that doesn't settle within a minute of racking the weight.

**Stop the session entirely:** repeated regurgitation; persistent chest pain or pressure; nausea or vomiting; pain that's worse when you lie down afterwards.

**Stop training and see a doctor promptly:** difficulty or pain swallowing; food feeling stuck; vomiting blood or material that looks like coffee grounds; black or tarry stools; unintentional weight loss; chest pain that spreads to the jaw, neck or arm, or comes with breathlessness or sweating. The last one is a call-emergency-services symptom, not a wait-and-see one — chest pain during exertion is cardiac until proven otherwise, and a reflux history does not exempt you from that.

### If symptoms get worse over the block

Drop the dumbbell RDL first — it's the highest-pressure movement remaining. Then drop the leg press. Then reduce the cable crunch load and add reps. If symptoms persist after removing all three, the problem isn't the program and it needs medical attention rather than further exercise substitution.

## Evidence grading

Every non-obvious claim in this document, sorted by how much weight it can bear.

### Established — multiple controlled trials or meta-analyses agree

- **Volume and hypertrophy are dose-related up to a point.** Meta-regression across 67 studies and 2,058 participants found a minimum effective dose near 4 fractional weekly sets per muscle and no clear plateau in the primary model, though with diminishing returns per added set. Separately, a moderate-vs-high comparison found no difference between 12–20 and 20+ weekly sets for quadriceps and biceps. The honest reading: more is generally better until recovery breaks, the curve flattens, and the exact ceiling is individual. This is why the program sits at 10–15 effective sets for most regions rather than chasing 25.
- **Proximity to failure matters; reaching failure is not required.** Training close to momentary failure meaningfully improves hypertrophy versus training far from it. The marginal benefit of the last rep is small and the fatigue cost is not.
- **Full range of motion beats shortened-range partials.**
- **Static stretching produces acute force reductions** when held long, with the effect largely gone within \~10 minutes or after ramp sets.
- **Stretching increases range of motion**, mostly via stretch tolerance rather than permanent tissue lengthening, and the gains regress when you stop.
- **Stretching does not reliably prevent injury or reduce soreness.** Repeatedly tested, repeatedly unsupported.
- **Regional hypertrophy within a muscle is real.** Different exercises grow different portions of the pec, quadriceps and hamstrings differently.
- **Raised intra-abdominal pressure increases reflux episodes**, and maximal Valsalva produces the highest intra-abdominal pressures in resistance training.

### Reasonable biomechanical inference — mechanism is sound, direct evidence is thin

- Overhead positions lengthen the triceps long head, and incline-bench positions lengthen the biceps long head, so those positions should preferentially stimulate them. Sound, but the direct hypertrophy comparisons are few.
- Elbow path (tucked vs flared) shifts rowing emphasis between lat and scapular retractors. Supported by EMG and moment-arm reasoning; not by longitudinal hypertrophy trials.
- Reclining the leg extension backrest lengthens rectus femoris and raises its share.
- Neutral and pronated grips raise brachialis and brachioradialis share in elbow flexion.
- Upper and lower rectus abdominis respond somewhat differently to spinal flexion vs pelvic tilt.
- Leaning the torso forward on hip abduction biases gluteus medius over TFL.
- Wide-stance, deep-hip-flexion work loads adductor magnus as a hip extensor.

### Where I overstated something

**Lengthened partials.** I wrote in Push B that stretched-range partials produce "at least as much hypertrophy as full range." That's stronger than the evidence supports. The accurate version: lengthened partials beat shortened partials consistently, and are roughly **equivalent** to full-range training — superior in some calf and quadriceps studies, no different in a 2025 trial of trained lifters' arms, and one vote-counting analysis argues full ROM should still be the default. They're a reasonable way to add two low-cost sets after full-range work, which is how they're programmed. They are not a superior method and I shouldn't have implied otherwise.

### Bodybuilding convention — widely believed, poorly supported

Used sparingly in this program and never as a load-bearing justification: grip width on pulldowns changing lat recruitment; toe position on calf raises shifting gastrocnemius head emphasis; toe-out stance targeting the VMO; adduction past the midline developing "inner chest"; squeezing at peak contraction producing extra growth.

### Speculation — stated as such or avoided

Any claim that a specific exercise isolates one head of a muscle. Any claim that a rep range uniquely produces "density" or "separation" as opposed to size. Any claim about optimal set counts to the precision of a single set.

### Things I was careful not to claim

That stretching prevents injury or joint pain. That soreness indicates growth. That any muscle head can be individually isolated. That more exercises automatically produce more development. That every exercise requires multiple warm-up sets. That removing deadlifts and heavy squats will prevent hernia progression — only that it reduces a known symptom trigger.
